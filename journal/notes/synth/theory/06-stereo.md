### Two-image formation — the stereo configuration and the depth it recovers

The single image pinned every visible point's bearing and discarded its range — [[05-single-image#What a single image preserves and destroys|the ray collapses]]. A second calibrated camera recovers that range: two known bearings to one point intersect at a single depth. The rest of the section pins down the geometry that turns this intersection into a one-dimensional search — the rectified configuration and the disparity it produces — then takes stock: what the second view adds (depth), what it leaves unchanged (bearing, orientation), and what now differs across the pair (disparity, intensity).

#### The canonical configuration
The **canonical (rectified) stereo configuration** is two identical pinholes with parallel optical axes and coplanar image planes, separated by a horizontal **baseline** $b$ ([[sze-cv#Stereo epipolar geometry & rectification (§12.1, printed p.753–756)|Szeliski §12.1]]). Each camera centre sees a **frustum** — the pyramid of rays through its image rectangle — and a world point can be matched only where the two frusta **intersect**; the band lying in one frustum but not the other is monocular, has no correspondence, and yields no depth. Real rigs are never perfectly coplanar, so the images are first **rectified**: warped by a homography (from the calibrated [[05-single-image#The perspective camera model|epipolar geometry]]) so that [[sze-cv#Stereo epipolar geometry & rectification (§12.1, printed p.753–756)|corresponding epipolar lines become horizontal image rows]]. After rectification the canonical geometry holds by construction; verged or widely-separated rigs that resist rectification fall back on the plane sweep below.

#### Epipolar geometry, disparity, and why a match needs high $I_x$
Calibration confines the match for a left pixel to its **epipolar line** in the right image — the intersection of the right image plane with the epipolar plane through both centres and the point ([[sze-cv#Stereo epipolar geometry & rectification (§12.1, printed p.753–756)|Szeliski §12.1]]); rectification makes that line the **same row**, collapsing the $2$D search to a $1$D scanline. Along it the match reduces to one number, the **disparity** $d=x_L-x_R$, tied to depth by the rectified law $d=fb/Z$: disparity is inversely proportional to range, strictly positive for finite depth and zero only at infinity, and so depth precision degrades as $Z^2$ — precisely $\sigma_Z=(Z^2/fb)\,\sigma_d$ for a given matching uncertainty $\sigma_d$. This sets the rig's central design **trade-off**: a wider baseline $b$ shrinks the depth error, but enlarges the inter-view appearance change and the half-occluded monocular band, so geometric accuracy is bought against matching difficulty and occlusion ([[sze-cv#Stereo epipolar geometry & rectification (§12.1, printed p.753–756)|Szeliski §12.7]]). Because the search runs along the baseline direction, only gradient *across* that direction localises the match — the [[05-single-image#Image edges|aperture problem]] specialised to the scanline: a horizontal-baseline match needs high $I_x$, and a purely horizontal edge (high $I_y$ alone) carries no disparity.

#### What the second view recovers, preserves, and holds constant
- **recovered** — **depth/range**, by triangulating the two bearings (the loss the single image flagged);
- **preserved** — **bearing** in each camera, exactly as in one view;
- **constant across both views** — the **orientation** of a world triangle images with the same handedness in both cameras, as long as its face is front-facing (or back-facing) in *both* ([[contesse#Orientation under projection (§2)|Contesse §2]]); the front↔back transition is itself a visual event, where the face edge turns into an occluding contour;
- **differs between views** — the disparity (which is what carries the depth), and the raw pixel **intensities**: per-camera gain, vignetting ($\cos^4\alpha$ fall-off) and slight-viewpoint non-Lambertian effects separate a point's two image values by an affine map $g=af+b$. This is why stereo matching scores with **zero-mean normalised cross-correlation** — invariant to that gain and bias — rather than raw SSD ([[sze-cv#Stereo similarity measures — SSD vs NCC, gain/bias invariance (§12.3.1, printed p.763–765; NCC eq. 9.11 p.561; gain/bias eq. 3.3 p.112)|Szeliski §12.3.1]]).

#### The occluding contour and frontier points
The occluding contour $\gamma$ behaves differently across the rig. Its surface source, the **contour generator** $\Gamma$, is the locus where the visual ray grazes the surface ([[hartley-mulview-geom#Images of smooth surfaces — contour generator (book p.200, §8, Def 8.8)|Hartley, Def 8.8]]; [[cip-vis-mot#Contour generator and apparent contour (paper p.1106)|Cipolla, eq. 2.1]]) — and that locus depends on the camera centre, so the two cameras graze the surface along **two different curves**. A generic contour point therefore has *no* stereo correspondence: the surface point imaged at the left silhouette simply is not on the right camera's contour generator. The exception is the **frontier point**, where the two contour generators cross ([[cip-vis-mot#Frontier points — fixed surface points visible in two views (paper p.1105)|Cipolla, frontier points]]): one fixed surface point lies on both grazing curves, so it appears in both images and triangulates like any other stereo match. Frontier points are the only depth a rig can read off a smooth, untextured contour.

#### Two routes to depth
With the geometry fixed, depth follows in one of two ways. **Correspondence**: match pixels along the scanline and invert $d=fb/Z$ — accurate wherever texture supplies the high $I_x$ a match needs. **Plane sweep**: hypothesise a family of depth planes, warp each camera onto them by the induced homography, and keep the depth that maximises photoconsistency ([[sze-cv#Plane sweep — depth without pre-rectification (§12.1.2, printed p.757–758)|Szeliski §12.1.2]]) — no explicit match, and it tolerates the verged configurations rectification cannot. 

One degenerate scene bounds the picture: if the world is a single plane, the two views are related by one global homography ([[hartley-mulview-geom#Plane induces a homography between two views (§13.1, book p.326–327)|Hartley & Zisserman, §13]]) — a different homography from the rectifying one, and the case where correspondence is fixed globally rather than searched per pixel.
